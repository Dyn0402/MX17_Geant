Finding 1Assumption A1 is refuted by your own data

The plan's assumption A1 (§1) is that the ESL resistive strips are tied to the HV/ground bus at both y-ends of the active area. The plan also lists V4 as an acceptance test: the predicted late-time drain must land within a factor of about two of the measured τg = 5.3–7.3 µs, “else revisit A1”.

A strip of length L grounded at both ends and obeying the sheet diffusion equation has slowest mode sin(πy/L), so its drain constant is τ = L²/(π²D) with D = 1/(ρsc′). With L = 412 mm that gives:

1.0 MΩ/sq8.12 ms×128911.5 mm
2.0 MΩ/sq16.24 ms×25788.1 mm
5.0 MΩ/sq40.61 ms×64465.1 mm

A1 misses by three orders of magnitude

Across the entire resistivity scan, A1 predicts milliseconds where you measure microseconds. For the strips to be the drain path, they would have to be grounded every 5–16 mm, not every 412 mm. This is not a marginal tension that a parameter tweak absorbs — it is a factor of 600 to 6000.

Three candidate explanations, in the order I would test them:

  1. The measured τg is not sheet transport at all. It may be the pad-plane / front-end RC. This is the cheapest to check and, to me, the most likely: a microsecond is a very natural electronics time constant and a very unnatural one for diffusion across 41 cm of 1 MΩ/sq paste.
  2. There is a conductive path at a much finer pitch than the active width — a grounding grid, or the paste contacting bus copper through vias at a few-mm pitch. That would be a fabrication detail nobody has written down. Note this is exactly §12 item 4, which the plan lists as an open fab-side question.
  3. ρs is far below the scanned range. To reach 6.3 µs through 412 mm strips you would need ρs around 1 kΩ/sq, which is not a resistive-paste value at all.

What I did about it: the solver takes tau_drain_s as an explicit input rather than deriving it from A1. The production scan should carry it as a nuisance parameter pinned to your measurement while the mechanism gets sorted out. That keeps the first-principles discipline honest — the drain is now visibly an input we do not understand, not a derived number hiding a wrong assumption.

Finding 2The X/Y sharing asymmetry falls out of geometry alone

This is the headline the plan asks for in §1: the measured detector shares charge differently in its two views (τY ≈ 410 ns against τX ≈ 230 ns, kY 1.8–2.9), and the simulation must reproduce that “from geometry alone — it is a headline validation target”.

It does. Nothing was tuned: the only inputs are the confirmed strip geometry (550 µm wide, 250 µm gaps, running along y), the 150 µm gap, and a resistivity from the middle of the scan.

Finding 3The 31.2 mm beat is large, and it lives only in the X view

The 800 µm ESL pitch against the 780 µm pad pitch means the registration between them advances 20 µm per pad and repeats after LCM = 31.2 mm — 39 strips to 40 pads. The plan flags this as a prediction to look for in data. Two things came out of measuring it.

The prompt kernel has exactly zero beat. At t = 0+ the sheet has not had time to conduct, so it behaves as a plain dielectric, σs(x) does not enter the equations at all, and the response is translation invariant. The beat is purely a property of the time-dependent part. My first attempt at this figure measured the prompt kernel and correctly showed a flat line; that was the figure being wrong, not the detector being boring.

Once the sheet conducts, the modulation is very large:

Time after the charge landsSignal retained, minmaxpeak-to-peak modulation
30 ns0.5620.91549.2 %
100 ns0.2930.877112.8 %
300 ns0.1530.841182.8 %

A pad sitting over conducting paste is strongly coupled to the sheet and its signal drains quickly; a pad sitting over a gap is coupled to charge that has nowhere to go and holds its signal. At 100 ns the difference between the best and worst pad phase exceeds the mean.

A sharp, cheap test

A whole X channel is one column of pads, so every pad in it shares the same x and therefore the same strip phase — the beat survives. A Y channel is a row spanning all x, so it averages over all 40 phases and the beat washes out. The prediction is a 31.2 mm periodic modulation in the X view and none in the Y view. That is a distinctive signature, it needs no new data, and if it is absent at anything like this amplitude then something in the assumed strip geometry or registration is wrong — which would itself be worth knowing.

Finding 4The checkerboard is real — and a channel is a comb, not a strip

T2 turned out to be answerable in the time left, and it changes the response model, so it is worth reading before the solver section.

The plan (§3, and §12 item 7) asks whether the pads alternate checkerboard between the two bus layers, and suggests looking at the via pattern. The vias do not answer it. Both bus layers put an annular ring on all 512×512 pads — on the 0.78 mm grid to 0.0 µm — because the via is a plated through hole running from physical L4 to L7 and rings every layer it passes.

What answers it is the 0.1 mm connector stub: a 390 µm trace, exactly half the pad pitch, from a pad's ring out to the bus line. A pad belongs to a layer's channel if and only if that layer draws a stub at it.

Bus layerConnector stubs(col+row) parityBus directionSo a channel is…
L5 “TrackY”131072 = 512×256all evenalong x (132279 h / 1973 v)a row → measures y
L6 “TrackX”131072 = 512×256all oddalong y (1942 h / 132248 v)a column → measures x

Zero overlap between the two sets, and 262030 of the 262144 pads assigned — the 114 unaccounted for are Y-layer stubs on the boundary column whose start point lands just outside the active window, a 0.04% edge effect rather than a competing assignment.

This changes the channel model

A channel is every other pad along its line — 256 pads on a 1.56 mm pitch, a comb — not 512 pads on a 0.78 mm pitch forming a solid strip. Its weighting potential is therefore a comb too. The drive patterns currently in wpot.py build solid lines, so the channel-level kernels must be rebuilt on the comb before any sharing fraction is quoted against the §9 targets.

The single-pad results in this report are unaffected: they are per-pad, and a channel is a linear sum over its pads. But the beat prediction above gets more interesting — a comb of 1.56 mm pitch against an 800 µm strip pitch is a second, much shorter beat sitting underneath the 31.2 mm one.

S1The solver, and one deviation from the plan

The physics is extended Ramo–Shockley for a resistive electrode (Riegler 2016). Driving channel n at Vw and grounding everything else, each lateral Fourier mode of the stack gives an areal capacitance and a drive coupling:

C(k) = ε₀ k coth(k g) + ε₀ ε_r k coth(k d)
S(k) = ε₀ ε_r k / sinh(k d)

Charge conservation on the sheet then fixes both ends of the problem. The sheet charge cannot jump, so V(0+) = S/C · Vw — the prompt, pure-dielectric solution. And the sheet relaxes from there toward zero, i.e. toward behaving as a grounded conductor. That decay is the signal.

In the small-k limit C(k) → ε₀(1/g + εr/d) = c′, and the relaxation becomes an exact Gaussian diffusion kernel with σ²(t) = 2t/(ρsc′) — which is the closed form the plan quotes, and the solver reproduces the plan's own sanity numbers: c′ = 4.72×10⁻⁷ F/m² (plan: ~5×10⁻⁷) and D = 2.12 m²/s at 1 MΩ/sq (plan: ~2.0).

The deviation: no truncation parameter

The plan proposes expanding the patterned sheet in Bloch modes and truncating at |m| ≤ M, starting at M = 12 with a convergence test. I did something else, and I think it is strictly better.

Working on a periodic box whose length is an exact multiple of both lateral pitches — the 31.2 mm superperiod — makes the mode coupling exactly block-diagonal by residue class of the mode index. There is no truncation: the blocks are closed. The only approximation left is the grid's own Nyquist cut, which converges by refining a single number (nx) instead of two. Each block is solved by symmetric eigendecomposition, so time propagation is by exact exponentials with no stepping error, as §3 requires.

One consequence is worth stating because it is physics, not numerics: the coupling operator has a genuine null space, and it must not be regularised away. A mode supported entirely on the insulating gaps has zero eigenvalue and never decays. That is the correct statement that charge landing in a 250 µm gap has no DC path to ground, and it is a large part of why the two views behave differently.

V1 passing at 8.6×10⁻¹⁶ — machine precision — is the load-bearing one. It exercises the block assembly, the similarity transform, the eigendecomposition and the propagator all at once against an answer known analytically, so a sign error or a mis-indexed Fourier coefficient could not survive it.

Two of these tests were wrong when I first wrote them, and both times the solver was right. V2 originally asserted that the strips drain to ground; they do not and must not, because with no y-gradient and insulating gaps on both sides there is nowhere for the charge to go. V4b originally expected a single exponential. Both are noted in the source so the reasoning is not lost.

A second prediction, from the drain term

A uniform leak conductance gives mode k a drain rate g/C(k). At the 250 µm gap scale C(k) is 2.2× its k→0 value, so charge stranded in the gaps drains about 2.2× slower than charge on the strips. The late-time tail should be two-component, where the current τg fits assume a single exponential. Refitting the existing rc_line_step2.py data with two components would be cheap and would test this directly.
